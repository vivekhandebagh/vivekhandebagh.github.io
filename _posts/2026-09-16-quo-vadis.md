 My lips kept murmuring _quo vadis_ in a rhythm that matched my gait. The words laid in my mind with a haunting sense of heaviness. Where was I going? I didn't know.

![qv](/assets/images/quo-vadis.jpg)

> Domine, quo vadis?
> Romam eo iterum crucifigi.

> Lord, where are you going?
> I am going to Rome to be crucified again.

This is a legendary scene in the apocryphal _Acts of Peter_. After the crucifixion of Christ, Peter had found his way out of Rome in escape from the persecution. He finds himself at the crossroads of _Via Appia_ when Christ appears to him, walking in the exact opposite direction.  


There's an immediate, surface level reading of courage that I grasped and respected. But my intuition knew that there must be a deeper reading, since I had enough meta-cognition to realize that the weight with which these words struck me meant that there was some latent internal tension that it addressed.

---------------------------------------

I don't think this scene is about cowardice since it wasn't purely cowardice that drove Peter out of Rome. It was actually his brethren and his followers of Christ that made the very rational argument that it would be imperative for Peter, who was one of the important leaders of the early Christian movement, to not be killed. Since only if he had his life would he be able to continue to spread the Gospel, teach, convert, reorganize, and appoint successors. For a consequentialist, this is the right course of action and it's probably the decision that would compound.

The same logic is found today: I see a lot of problematic things in society and I want to help make the world a better place. But I don't have enough capacity yet. I need to acquire capital, skills, and influence. Later, once I have more, I will have the capacity to do vastly more good. This is the essence of the argument that maximizing goodness throughput prerequisites accumulating capital. Instead of charitably spending \$1000 today, invest it and grow it to \$10,000 tomorrow so you can do 10x as much good.

People already debate this, and the most common counterargument is that this strategy fails due to infinite regress, that it wouldn't make sense to spend the \$10,000 when you can grow it to \$100,000 so as to do 100x more good, but later.

But there's a reframe that this story gave me. Christ walks past Peter towards Rome, bearing the cross on his shoulder- if you don't go, I will. Someone needs to bear the cost of suffering.


The calculations of optimal philantropic strategy heavily under estimate the fact that the world does not pause during the accumulation phase. The suffering you aim to eliminate, later, won't pause for you until you're ready with the resources to start tackling the problem. Growing philantropic capital with some expectation of future goodness-flow must be additionally discounted by the weighted average cost of moral capital. Your investment is being financed by a world where you expect someone else will bear the urgent burdens, while you reserve yourself for a greater future usefulness.

I am not saying that this financing is never justified. I've just realized that it is definitely not cheap.
